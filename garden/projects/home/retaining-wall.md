
### **1\. Columbia River Basalt Slabs (A-Split Dry-Stack)**
  ![[retaining-wall-20261122-01.png]]


> * **Synopsis:** A heavy, organic gravity wall built with regional dark charcoal and iron-stained quarry stone. It steps down naturally with the sidewalk slope, creating deep horizontal shadow lines that blend natively into Pacific Northwest landscaping.  
> * **Contractor Cost:** **\$7,500 – \$10,500**  
  * *Drivers:* Stone weight (\~4 tons of split basalt at \$450–\$550/ton), flatbed crane delivery, and intensive manual mason labor to shape, level, and interlock every irregular course without mortar.  
> * **Longevity:** **100+ Years (Indefinite)**  
  * Natural stone cannot rot, rust, or degrade. A dry-stack system allows water to seep freely through joints, completely eliminating hydrostatic pressure blowout.  
> * **Key Considerations:**  
  * *Tolerances:* Basalt faces are irregular. While the base sits 6 inches back, protruding rock faces will vary, making your gravel planting trough look more rustic and uneven.  
  * *Leaf Cleanup:* Fallen basswood leaves settle into rock crevices and need to be blown out regularly to prevent them from breaking down into compost between the stones.

### **2\. Structural Corten Weathering Steel (3/16" Plate)**

![[retaining-wall-20261122-02.png]]

> * **Synopsis:** A razor-thin, modern vertical profile with clean 90-degree corners. Over its first two seasons, it oxidizes into a rich, matte amber-rust patina that contrasts sharply against dark house siding and pale gravel.  
> * **Contractor Cost:** **\$6,800 – \$9,200**  
  * *Drivers:* Heavy structural plate (14-gauge lawn edging cannot hold this hill; it requires true 3/16" plate or engineered panels), custom corner bending/welding, soil tiebacks (deadmen), and post anchors set into concrete.  
> * **Longevity:** **50 – 70+ Years**  
  * The dense exterior oxide layer seals the steel against progressive rust. In Portland's wet climate, it weathers quickly, but 3/16" thickness takes decades to suffer structural loss.  
> * **Key Considerations:**  
  * *Footprint Efficiency:* At just 3/16" thick, it consumes virtually zero bed depth, preserving the maximum possible width for your upper planting terrace and keeping the 6-inch sidewalk/driveway buffer completely open.  
  * *Rust Runoff:* As it cures during the first 12–18 months of rain, runoff water carries rust particulate. Your gravel buffer is crucial here to catch the drip line and prevent permanent orange stains on your sidewalk concrete.  
  * *Leaf Cleanup:* Basswood leaves blow cleanly off the smooth steel top edge without catching.

### **3\. Rough-Hewn Ground-Contact Timber (4×6 / 6×6)**

![[retaining-wall-20261122-03.png]]
> * **Synopsis:** Stacked heavy Douglas fir or hem-fir timbers finished in deep carbon-black exterior oil. It features visible saw marks, natural knotting, and checking, offering a grounded, modern-craftsman look that echoes horizontal lap siding.  
> * **Contractor Cost:** **\$3,500 – \$5,200**  
  * *Drivers:* Lowest material outlay (\~\$500–\$750) and fast mechanical assembly. Timbers are rapidly cut, stacked, lagged with structural TimberLOK screws, and pinned to the subgrade with rebar stakes.  
> * **Longevity:** **15 – 25 Years**  
  * Even with modern AWPA UC4A ground-contact pressure treatment and non-woven geotextile behind the wall, heavy exposure to damp Portland soil and persistent winter moisture will eventually cause checking, warping, and fungal decay.  
> * **Key Considerations:**  
  * *Budget Preservation:* This option costs roughly half of basalt or structural steel, leaving thousands of dollars free for other priorities.  
  * *Maintenance:* The black exterior stain or timber oil will need a wash and reapplication every 3–4 years to maintain its deep color against UV and rain wash.  
  * *Wall Thickness:* A 4×6 (3.5" bed depth) or 6×6 (5.5" bed depth) takes up noticeable room along the terrace perimeter compared to steel.

### **Concrete Next Steps**

> 1. **Decide the Lifespan vs. Budget Trade-Off:** Determine whether you want a permanent 50+ year masonry/steel asset, or if a \~20-year timber wall at roughly half the total investment makes more sense for your overall budget.  
> 2. **Collect Material Samples Locally:**  
   * Stop by a local yard like Portland Rock & Landscape Supply to view split Columbia River basalt slabs in person.  
   * Pick up an 8-foot piece of pressure-treated 4×6 ground-contact lumber from a local lumberyard to test a sample of carbon black/iron timber stain against your house siding and staircase.  
> 3. **Obtain Quotes with Fixed Specifications:** If hiring out, provide contractors with the exact 26-foot run, 2.5-foot maximum height, 6-inch dual setback, and integrated stair layout to get firm, apples-to-apples bids.

# **Plantings**

The combination of a **north-facing slope** and a **broadleaf street tree across the sidewalk** still creates specific microclimate factors that will impact the densely packed conifer concept:

---

### **The Reality of North-Facing \+ Hell Strip Basswood**

* **Direct Sun vs. Open Sky Exposure:** A north-facing yard primarily receives ambient, indirect sky light rather than direct overhead beams. While the basswood across the sidewalk doesn’t cast dead overhead shadow, its dense summer canopy acts like a tall curtain to the north, cutting off a substantial chunk of reflected northern light from late spring through early autumn.  
* **Leaf Drop Dynamics:** Even planted across the sidewalk, mature basswoods drop copious, wide leaves that will blow directly across the concrete and collect against the retaining wall face, the buffer trench, and inside dense plant crowns.  
* **Moisture Retention in Winter:** Without direct southern solar exposure to dry the bed in winter, dark stone mulch and shaded soil stay continuously wet from November through March.

---

### **Plant Survival & Performance in This Setting**

| Conifer Type | Performance in This Bed | Practical Reality |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Blue Spruces & True Pines** (*Picea pungens*, *Pinus mugo*) | **Poor** | Need intense direct sun. In ambient north light, they tend to turn dull greenish-gray, shed interior needles, and thin out noticeably over 2–3 seasons. |
| **Dwarf Hemlocks** (*Tsuga canadensis 'Moon Frost'*, *'Jervis'*) | **Excellent** | Naturally evolved for understories and cool, northern aspects; foliage stays lush and bright. |
| **Plum Yews & Japanese Yews** (*Cephalotaxus*, *Taxus*) | **Excellent** | Thrives in pure north light; deep green color remains vibrant without sun scorch. |
| **Russian Arborvitae** (*Microbiota decussata*) | **Very Good** | Handles partial and ambient shade far better than junipers; provides horizontal layering without thinning out. |
| **Compact Broadleaf Evergreens** (*Sarcococca*, *Mahonia nervosa*) | **Excellent** | Thick, glossy foliage sheds fallen basswood leaves easily and handles damp Portland winters without fungal needle cast. |

---

### **Recommended Layout Tweaks for the Concept**

1. **Swap Sun-Hungry Cones for Shade Lovers:** Keep the varied silhouette from the renderings, but anchor the tallest focal points with **Tsuga canadensis** (Dwarf Hemlock) and **Taxus cuspidata 'Nana'** instead of blue spruces or dwarf pines.  
2. **Loosen the Planting Density:** Space the specimens 4–6 inches farther apart than shown in the tight rendering. This prevents fallen basswood leaves from getting trapped in a solid mat of foliage, allowing a leaf blower to clear them easily in autumn.  
3. **Use the 6-Inch Gravel Trench as a Leaf Trap:** Keep the ground-level gravel buffer relatively open with low, leathery ferns (*Polystichum*) or hardy sedums so blowing street leaves don't get trapped against the wall face.

### **Proven Dwarf Conifers for North-Facing Shade**

True conifers that hold dense needles and rich color without direct southern sun, while shedding fallen basswood leaves cleanly:

* **Taxus cuspidata 'Nana' (Dwarf Japanese Yew):** Upright, dense architectural column (3–4 ft) with deep, dark-emerald needles. Thrives in full north light and dense shade where pines and spruces thin out.  
* **Cephalotaxus harringtonia 'Duke Gardens' (Plum Yew):** Layered, spreading vase habit (2–3 ft tall, 3–4 ft wide) with long, glossy, deep-green needles resembling miniature hemlock boughs. Exceptionally heat- and shade-tolerant.  
* **Tsuga canadensis 'Moon Frost' (Dwarf Eastern Hemlock):** Compact mounding globe (2–3 ft) with soft foliage and frosted, blush-white/pale silver spring tips that brighten dark corners. Naturally evolved for forest understories.  
* **Microbiota decussata (Russian Arborvitae):** Low, feathery tiered groundcover (10–12 inches tall, spreading 3–4 ft). Tolerates open shade significantly better than creeping junipers, turning from lime green to rich bronze-plum in winter.  
* **Podocarpus nivalis 'Jalako Red' (Alpine Totara):** Tough, low-mounding conifer (18–24 inches) with small, olive-green needles whose tips blush bronze-red in cold weather; handles ambient north light without stretching.


### **Architectural Ferns for Contrast & Texture**

Ferns bridge the gap between heavy wood timbers and needle foliage, tolerating summer dryness once established:

* **Polystichum munitum (Western Sword Fern):** Native, leathery, upright evergreen fronds (2–3 ft). Bulletproof against summer dry shade and easy to clear around during autumn leaf drop.  
* **Polystichum acrostichoides (Christmas Fern):** Deep green, glossy, clumping evergreen fronds (18–24 inches) that stay upright through winter and tolerate lean soils.  
* **Dryopteris erythrosora 'Brilliance' (Autumn Fern):** Semi-evergreen arching fronds whose spring unfurling starts coppery-orange before maturing to lustrous dark green, providing a bright color contrast against dark-stained timber.  
* **Adiantum aleuticum (Western Maidenhair Fern):** Delicate, horizontal fan-like fronds on wiry black stems. Thrives in cool, north-facing pockets shielded from wind and harsh afternoon sun.

### **Broadleaf Accents, Living Mulch & Succulents**

Companions that add glossy leaf forms, winter flowers, and crevice coverage along the 6-inch buffer:

* **Sarcococca confusa (Sweet Box):** Compact, mounding broadleaf evergreen (2–3 ft) with mirror-gloss deep green leaves and intensely fragrant white winter blooms that scent the walkway from January to March.  
* **Mahonia nervosa (Cascade Oregon Grape):** Native, low-growing architectural shrub (1–2 ft) with matte holly-like leaflets that take on purplish winter hues; extremely drought-tolerant under shade.  
* **Vancouveria hexandra (Inside-Out Flower):** Native deciduous groundcover forming a delicate, duck-foot patterned green carpet (8–12 inches) that acts as a soft living mulch beneath the conifers.  
* **Sedum spathulifolium 'Cape Blanco' (Broadleaf Stonecrop):** Native, powdery silver-blue succulent rosettes that thrive tucked directly into the 6-inch pea gravel buffer and outer timber crevices where drainage is sharp.  
* **Sempervivum tectorum (Hens and Chicks):** Tight, geometric succulent clusters for the sunny outer lip of the gravel strip, handling winter rain provided the gravel base drains freely.


### **Layered Planting Layout**

| Zone | Primary Species | Purpose |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Back Tier (High Grade)** | *Taxus cuspidata 'Nana'* & *Sarcococca confusa* | Architectural height, winter fragrance, and deep green backdrop against house siding. |
| **Mid-Terrace (Terrace Bed)** | *Cephalotaxus 'Duke Gardens'*, *Tsuga 'Moon Frost'*, & *Autumn Fern* | Contrasting textures (soft weeping needles vs. arching coppery fronds); sheds fallen leaves easily. |
| **Front Timber Edge** | *Microbiota decussata* & *Cascade Oregon Grape* | Spreading evergreen skirts that drape slightly over the top timber run without trapping debris. |
| **6-Inch Buffer Channel** | *Cape Blanco Stonecrop*, *Sempervivum*, & *Maidenhair Fern* | Low-profile textural border along the sidewalk and driveway that stays clear of foot traffic. |

### **Execution Checklist**

1. **Verify Soil Volume Behind the Timber:** Ensure the upper terrace retains at least 14–16 inches of organic planting mix over a 4-inch base of 3/4-minus gravel before planting the *Taxus* and *Cephalotaxus* root balls.  
2. **Set Gravel Buffer Depths:** Keep the 6-inch perimeter trough filled with 2–3 inches of clean 3/8" pea gravel over landscape fabric, pocketing soil only where individual *Sedum* and *Sempervivum* clusters are placed.  
3. **Establish Watering Schedule:** Water deeply once a week during the first summer to establish the root systems before tapering off to natural rainfall and occasional dry-spell soaks.
